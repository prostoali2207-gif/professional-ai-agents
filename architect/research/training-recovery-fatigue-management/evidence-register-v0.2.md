# Training Recovery & Fatigue Management — evidence register v0.2

Date: 2026-09-21
Status: design evidence; not a qualification result.

## Source hierarchy

Primary/consensus and systematic-review evidence is preferred. Practitioner heuristics are allowed only when labeled as operational heuristics and never promoted into clinical cutoffs.

## Sources

### E01 — Meeusen et al. 2013
Prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of the overtraining syndrome: joint consensus statement of ECSS and ACSM.
PMID: 23247672
DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0b013e318279a10a
Use: distinguishes functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching and OTS; OTS/NFOR distinction is difficult and depends on clinical outcome/exclusion diagnosis.
Decision impact: the skill must not diagnose OTS from fatigue/readiness data.

### E02 — Bourdon et al. 2017
Monitoring Athlete Training Loads: Consensus Statement.
PMID: 28463642
DOI: 10.1123/IJSPP.2017-0208
Use: multidisciplinary load monitoring; internal/external load; monitoring is decision support, not one perfect metric.

### E03 — Saw, Main & Gastin 2016
Monitoring the athlete training response: subjective self-reported measures trump commonly used objective measures: a systematic review.
PMID: 26423706
DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2015-094758
Use: subjective measures were generally more sensitive/consistent to acute and chronic training load than commonly used objective measures.
Decision impact: subjective fatigue/sleep/stress can be useful monitoring evidence, but instrument quality matters; single-item ratings must not be treated as objectively calibrated physiology or as sole decision authority.

### E04 — Walsh et al. 2021 consensus
Sleep and the athlete: narrative review and expert consensus recommendations.
PMID: 33144349
DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2020-102025
Use: athlete sleep is individualized; one-size-fits-all sleep-duration prescriptions are not ideal; sleep inadequacy can impair performance.
Decision impact: store sleep trend and perceived sleep need/quality; do not use one universal sleep cutoff as a training-stop rule.

### E05 — Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019
How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy?
PMID: 30558493
Use: when volume is equated, higher vs lower weekly frequency did not meaningfully change hypertrophy.
Decision impact: frequency is primarily a distribution/recovery lever inside this skill, not an independent recovery score or universal target.

### E06 — Resistance training prescription network meta-analysis 2023
Resistance training prescription for muscle strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults.
PMCID: PMC10579494
Use: multiple resistance-training prescriptions improve strength/hypertrophy; load, sets and frequency interact.
Decision impact: fatigue management must preserve the hypertrophy/strength stimulus rather than simply minimizing effort.

### E07 — Proximity-to-failure systematic review/meta-analysis
Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy.
PMCID: PMC9935748
Use: momentary failure is not required for hypertrophy and can increase acute neuromuscular fatigue.
Decision impact: proximity-to-failure is a legitimate fatigue-control variable.

### E08 — Autoregulation systematic review/meta-analysis 2022
The Effect of Load and Volume Autoregulation on Muscular Strength and Hypertrophy.
PMID: 35038063
PMCID: PMC8762534
Use: RIR/RPE and velocity-based autoregulation are plausible tools; chronic outcomes are not uniformly superior to standardized prescription.
Decision impact: autoregulation is a tool, not a guarantee; use comparable performance evidence and uncertainty.

### E09 — RPE validity systematic review/meta-analysis 2022
Convergent Validity of Ratings of Perceived Exertion During Resistance Exercise in Healthy Participants.
PMID: 35000021
Use: RPE is a valid practical measure of exercise intensity/exertion with substantial heterogeneity.
Decision impact: RPE/RIR can support session interpretation, but should be contextualized and familiarized.

### E10 — HRV-guided training systematic review/meta-analysis 2021
Heart Rate Variability-Guided Training for Enhancing Cardiac-Vagal Modulation, Aerobic Fitness, and Endurance Performance.
PMID: 34639599
PMCID: PMC8507742
Use: methods vary; rolling averages and baseline choices matter; performance advantages are small/non-significant in reviewed endurance evidence.
Decision impact: HRV cannot be used as a universal daily readiness truth, especially for hypertrophy training.

### E11 — HRV-guided training critique 2026
Heart rate variability-guided endurance training: evaluating strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats for load prescription and adjustment.
PMID: 42724227
Use: HRV has limited specificity; proprietary metrics and opaque rule systems may oversimplify decisions; transparent multivariate frameworks are needed.
Decision impact: no proprietary recovery score hard gate.

### E12 — Consumer wearable sleep validation systematic review 2024
Accuracy of Fitbit Charge 4, Garmin Vivosmart 4, and WHOOP Versus Polysomnography.
PMID: 38557808
PMCID: PMC11004611
Use: consumer wearables are practical for longitudinal home use but accuracy, particularly sleep-stage classification, is a concern.
Decision impact: prefer coarse longitudinal patterns such as estimated sleep duration/timing over exact stage claims.

### E13 — Deload intervention 2024
Gaining more from doing less? The effects of a one-week deload period during supervised resistance training on muscular adaptations.
PMCID: PMC10809978
Use: a fixed one-week midpoint deload did not improve hypertrophy and was associated with worse lower-body strength outcomes than continuous training in that study.
Decision impact: reject mandatory calendar deloads as evidence-free universal policy.

### E14 — Deload intervention 2026
Effects of deload periods in resistance training on muscle hypertrophy and strength endurance in untrained young men using a randomized within-subject design.
PMCID: PMC13031491
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5
Use: reducing volume/frequency during deload periods did not appear to hinder hypertrophy or strength-endurance in that specific untrained population.
Decision impact: deloads can be a viable load-reduction tool, but the evidence does not justify one mandatory schedule for everyone.

### E15 — Jeffries et al. 2020
Athlete-Reported Outcome Measures for Monitoring Training Responses: A Systematic Review of Risk of Bias and Measurement Property Quality According to the COSMIN Guidelines.
PMID: 32957081
DOI: 10.1123/ijspp.2020-0386
Use: commonly used athlete-reported measures vary in measurement quality; single-item measures were frequently unvalidated, and modified nonvalidated items were common.
Decision impact: a one-item fatigue/readiness/wellness number is contextual self-report, not an objectively calibrated physiological measurement. Prefer consistent within-person wording/scales and trends, and preserve uncertainty.

### E16 — Damas, Libardi & Ugrinowitsch 2018
The development of skeletal muscle hypertrophy through resistance training: the role of muscle damage and muscle protein synthesis.
PMID: 29282529
DOI: 10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9
Use: resistance-training protocols can produce hypertrophy without substantial muscle damage; muscle damage is not established as the process that mediates or potentiates hypertrophy.
Decision impact: soreness/damage must not be used as proof of hypertrophy or as a target to maximize.

### E17 — Nosaka et al. 2003
Muscle Damage in Resistance Training — Is Muscle Damage Necessary for Strength Gain and Muscle Hypertrophy?
DOI: 10.5432/ijshs.1.1
Use: delayed-onset muscle soreness does not necessarily reflect the magnitude of muscle damage; damage/soreness are not indispensable for adaptation.
Decision impact: soreness is a local/contextual recovery signal, not a quantitative damage gauge or growth score.

## Evidence conflicts / uncertainty

1. Training frequency literature has evolved: earlier work sometimes favored higher frequency, while later volume-equated evidence finds little meaningful independent effect. Runtime decision: use frequency as a way to distribute tolerable weekly work when volume is the main hypertrophy driver.
2. Deload evidence is sparse and population/protocol specific. Runtime decision: triggered/conditional deload logic, not fixed-calendar dogma.
3. HRV evidence is primarily endurance-oriented and method-sensitive. Runtime decision: optional adjunct only, never sole determinant for resistance-training changes.
4. No validated single biomarker or readiness score uniquely distinguishes normal fatigue, under-recovery, NFOR or OTS in ordinary hypertrophy trainees. Runtime decision: multivariate longitudinal evidence plus escalation when clinical interpretation would be required.
5. Subjective monitoring can be sensitive to training response, but measurement quality is heterogeneous and many single-item wellness measures are not validated. Runtime decision: use subjective data as provenance-labeled context/trends, not as objective physiological truth.
6. Soreness and muscle damage are not established prerequisites or quantitative proxies for hypertrophy. Runtime decision: soreness may affect local training decisions but cannot prove growth or prescribe more/less training by itself.

## Retention classification

Stable embedded principles:
- trends beat isolated noisy observations for structural decisions;
- load and recovery must be interpreted together;
- one metric cannot diagnose systemic state;
- a single-item wellness/fatigue rating is contextual self-report, not calibrated physiology;
- soreness is not a growth score or reliable quantitative damage gauge;
- OTS is not a runtime self-diagnosis;
- deload is conditional, not mandatory;
- reversible smallest-change-first is preferred when evidence is ambiguous.

Reference/procedural:
- state schema;
- decision/judgment model;
- evaluation case families.

Live research:
- materially new consensus/guidelines;
- device-specific validation or algorithm claims;
- current medical guidance when escalation wording depends on it.

Escalate:
- medical diagnosis;
- persistent unexplained systemic deterioration;
- clinically significant sleep/illness/injury concerns.
