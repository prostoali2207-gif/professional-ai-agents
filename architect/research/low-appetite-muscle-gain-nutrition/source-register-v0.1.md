## Evidence synthesis decisions

### Protein
Use ~1.6 g/kg/day as the default **sufficiency target** for a generally healthy resistance-trained adult with constrained appetite, with an ordinary working range of roughly 1.6–2.0 g/kg/day. Do not force the upper end when it displaces needed energy or worsens satiety. Per-meal distribution is a secondary optimization.

### Energy surplus / rate of gain
Do not encode a large fixed surplus. Published evidence shows faster gain/larger surplus reliably raises fat-gain risk and has weak/inconsistent incremental hypertrophy evidence. Use conservative, feedback-controlled energy increments. The literature's 0.25–0.5% body-mass/week range is a reference/upper operating band scaled to training experience, **not a mandatory minimum**. If slower positive trend coexists with progressing training and adequate recovery, do not increase intake solely to hit a rate.

### Carbohydrate
Do not impose endurance-style carbohydrate targets on ordinary resistance training. Allocate carbohydrate after protein/fat adequacy according to total-energy need, preference, session volume, fed/fasted state, and observed performance. Increase priority for high-volume, glycogen-depleting, long, or twice-daily training.

### Fat
Keep fat within ordinary health-compatible athlete ranges and use it deliberately for energy density. Avoid chronic extreme restriction. Do not market dietary fat as a testosterone hack.

### Energy density
Energy enrichment is a legitimate low-volume tactic. Prefer adding calories to tolerated foods (oils/sauces, nut/seed pastes where suitable, full-fat dairy if tolerated, rice/pasta/bread/cereal, dried fruit, smoothies/shakes) rather than simply increasing plate volume.

### Timing/frequency
Daily sufficiency dominates. Timing/frequency are conditional tools for tolerance, protein distribution, and training performance. There is no universal requirement for six meals or a narrow anabolic window.

## Conflicts and unresolved evidence

- Optimal surplus magnitude is not well established; current evidence is sparse. Therefore the skill uses a control system, not false precision.
- Liquid calories may help some people practically, but evidence does not justify claiming liquids are universally less satiating.
- Exact carbohydrate needs for hypertrophy are uncertain and training-dependent.
- Exact micronutrient/fiber targets depend on age/sex/jurisdiction and should not be hard-coded beyond broad adequacy unless live/current reference values are required.
