## Evidence synthesis

### Supplements

Evidence must be ingredient-specific and outcome-specific. A plausible biochemical mechanism is insufficient. Prefer systematic reviews, authoritative fact sheets/position stands, and human trials for the target outcome.

For a generally healthy resistance-training adult:
- creatine monohydrate has the strongest evidence for increasing high-intensity training capacity and supporting lean-mass/strength adaptation when combined with resistance training;
- protein powder is primarily a convenience method for meeting protein needs, not a separate anabolic class;
- caffeine can improve some performance outcomes but raises sleep, anxiety, tachycardia and stimulant-stacking concerns; higher dose is not automatically better;
- beta-alanine is context-specific and not a general hypertrophy supplement;
- BCAA products usually add little when total high-quality protein is adequate;
- many multi-ingredient "test boosters", proprietary blends and novel compounds have weak efficacy evidence or uncertain identity.

Exact dose/form recommendations must come from the relevant current reference module/evidence and account for individual context.

### Product quality

A label and retail availability do not prove composition, efficacy, safety, legality in every jurisdiction, or anti-doping eligibility. For tested athletes, current batch/product risk controls and authoritative anti-doping references are required.

### PEDs

Non-medical AAS/PED use can improve muscle/strength outcomes, but that efficacy cannot be separated from material multisystem risk. Current evidence supports clinically important cardiovascular, endocrine/reproductive, hepatic and hematologic concerns. Observational uncertainty does not justify calling a regimen safe.

### Anti-doping

The WADA list is versioned and updated annually. Never answer a current prohibited-status question from stored memory when live authoritative verification is available.
