# Hypertrophy Training — evidence-backed programming reference

Version: 0.1
Source of provenance: `architect/research/hypertrophy-training/source-register-v0.1.md`

Load this reference when the decision requires deeper evidence than the compact skill invariants.

## Evidence hierarchy

Use current authoritative healthy-adult guidance as the default anchor, then systematic reviews/meta-analyses, then primary trials. Do not turn a population average into an individual law.

## Volume

Current evidence supports a positive dose-response between weekly set exposure and hypertrophy with diminishing returns. Strength also benefits from volume, with more pronounced diminishing returns.

Operational interpretation:
- start from the trainee's recent tolerated/effective work when available;
- do not force every muscle to a universal set target;
- do not add volume when performance is already progressing;
- consider a small volume increase only after adequate effort, technique, adherence and recovery make insufficient dose a plausible limiter;
- reduce volume when recent increases coincide with persistent performance/recovery degradation.

The ACSM 2026 overview reports enhanced hypertrophy at higher weekly volumes (including the aggregate >=10 sets/week signal). Treat that as population evidence, not a personal mandatory floor.

Sources: HT-S01, HT-S03, HT-S04, HT-S05.

## Load and repetition range

For exercise-specific maximal strength, heavier loading and practice specificity matter. For hypertrophy, similar growth can occur across broad loading zones when effort is sufficient.

Operational interpretation:
- priority strength lifts should include regular heavier practice appropriate to the lifter and technique;
- accessory/hypertrophy work can use a broad rep range;
- choose a load that supports stable ROM/technique, target-muscle work, useful effort and measurable progression;
- avoid very light/high-rep or very heavy/low-rep extremes by default when they create unnecessary discomfort, time or fatigue for the same hypertrophy target.

Sources: HT-S01, HT-S02, HT-S17.

## Proximity to failure

Momentary muscular failure is not established as universally superior for hypertrophy. Meta-regression suggests hypertrophy tends to improve as sets finish closer to failure, while failure and high-fatigue protocols increase acute fatigue.

Operational interpretation:
- prescribe sufficiently hard sets rather than requiring failure;
- use exercise/task-specific RIR targets;
- keep more margin on high-skill/high-consequence free-weight work;
- closer-to-failure work is more practical on stable machine/isolation exercises when recovery permits;
- when RIR accuracy is poor, calibrate on a stable safe movement and avoid false precision.

Sources: HT-S06–HT-S10.

## Frequency

Hypertrophy frequency is mainly useful for distributing volume and preserving set quality when weekly dose is matched. Strength also benefits from more frequent task-specific practice in aggregate evidence.

Operational interpretation:
- distribute weekly work so per-session quality and recovery remain acceptable;
- use enough exposure to practice priority strength lifts;
- do not prescribe a universal muscle frequency.

Sources: HT-S01, HT-S03.

## Exercise order

Strength gains are larger for exercises placed earlier when those exercises are the measured target. Hypertrophy is less clearly affected by order.

Operational interpretation:
- order by priority and fatigue interaction;
- "compound first" is not a universal rule;
- a priority isolation/local-hypertrophy target may go earlier when that is the actual goal.

Sources: HT-S01, HT-S13.

## Exercise selection and variation

Systematic variation can change regional hypertrophy and strength outcomes; excessive/random rotation can reduce measurability and adaptation.

Operational interpretation:
- keep productive exercises stable long enough to evaluate;
- change when target coverage, tolerance, equipment, motivation or progression requires it;
- establish a new baseline after substitution;
- preserve strength-specific lifts when the strength outcome is exercise-specific.

Sources: HT-S14.

## ROM / muscle length

Full ROM is a robust default when tolerable and appropriate. Some evidence favors training at longer muscle lengths for particular muscles/regions, but newer pooled evidence suggests effects are small and exercise-specific.

Operational interpretation:
- use a useful, tolerable ROM;
- do not sacrifice joint tolerance, setup stability or progression merely to chase "maximum stretch";
- lengthened partials are an option, not a universal upgrade.

Sources: HT-S15, HT-S16.

## Rest

Rest is a means to preserve the intended set output, not a hypertrophy ritual. In trained lifters, longer rest has outperformed short rest in at least one controlled trial; the ACSM overview does not find one rest prescription consistently decisive across outcomes.

Operational interpretation:
- rest until the next set can meet its intended load/reps/RIR/technique;
- shorten rest only when density is deliberately part of the task and does not sabotage priority output.

Source: HT-S12, contextualized by HT-S01.

## Fatigue and recovery

Closer-to-failure work, more volume and greater density raise acute fatigue. Subjective recovery/readiness can be useful longitudinal context but should not be used as a single decisive metric.

Operational interpretation:
- monitor performance, set effort, local recovery and subjective context together;
- identify the variable that most likely created the fatigue before reducing training;
- distinguish a hard session from a persistent deteriorating trend.

Sources: HT-S08, HT-S09, HT-S21–HT-S23.

## Deload

Evidence is sparse and form-dependent. Complete one-week cessation in one trained trial reduced strength gains relative to continuous training while not changing hypertrophy; a small 2026 untrained within-subject study found reduced volume/frequency deloads did not hinder adaptations.

Operational interpretation:
- no universal deload calendar;
- when accumulated fatigue is credible, reduce the fatigue-producing dose while preserving useful practice when possible;
- define a re-entry criterion;
- full cessation requires a specific reason rather than being the default.

Sources: HT-S18, HT-S19.

## Monitoring and progression

Raw set history is more useful than a single derived score. RIR/RPE, estimated 1RM, volume load, soreness and readiness all have measurement limitations.

Operational interpretation:
- compare standardized exposures;
- preserve raw load/reps/RIR and setup version;
- treat e1RM and tonnage as derived signals, not hypertrophy stimulus;
- require repeated evidence before declaring plateau or accumulated fatigue.

Sources: HT-S10, HT-S11, HT-S21.

## Safety boundary

The validated evidence boundary is healthy adults. Exercise screening should consider current activity, warning signs/symptoms or known disease, and intended intensity. This skill does not diagnose.

Source: HT-S20.
